Terminal
Overview Accept Stake How it works

Two contracts, and after deployment nobody owns either of them.

Terminal takes payments and skims 2.5%. Interchange mints the token, shares the fee, and holds the stakes. The address Terminal pays into was fixed at deployment with no setter — which is the difference between "the fee goes to stakers" and "the fee goes wherever the operator later decides."

One privileged action existed: naming the rail. It was used once and can never be used again. There is no owner after that.

What happens in one payment

A single transaction, three movements, no balance left behind.

StepWhat moves
Customer signs100.00 USDC leaves their wallet. The quoted price is the charged price — the fee is taken from it, never added on top, because a displayed price that isn't what you pay is a lie.
Merchant paid97.50 goes straight to their payout address. It never touches Terminal's balance, so there is nothing to hold, delay, or lose.
Fee routed2.50 passes through Terminal into Interchange in the same call. If Interchange ever refused it, the whole payment would revert — better a failed payment than a fee stranded somewhere with no way out.

How a stake earns

Two numbers do all the work: accFeePerShare and accEscPerShare. When a payment lands, each rises by its share divided by the total staked. Your claim is the difference between those numbers now and what they were when you last touched your stake.

Which means the thing people always worry about can't happen: staking after a fee arrives earns you nothing from it. Your starting point is set at the moment you stake, so you only ever collect from fees that came in after you did.

Fees that arrive when nobody is staked would have no denominator to divide by, so they're banked and released into the pool the moment someone stakes. Nothing is stranded.

The escrow, which is the interesting part

Rewards can be paid as esSWIPE — escrowed units with no transfer function at all. Not a modifier that could be bypassed; there is simply no code path that moves them. The only exit is converting to real SWIPE, linearly over a year.

And to convert, you must reserve an equal amount of staked SWIPE. Reserved stake can't be withdrawn until the conversion finishes.

1,000 staked · 400 esSWIPE converting

600 free to unstake
400 reserved

The reserved portion still earns fees the whole time. It just can't leave.

Nobody is slashed for leaving. Leaving is simply not available while you're mid-conversion — and cancelling returns the unconverted part to escrow and frees the stake immediately. A lock is easier to defend and harder to resent than a forfeiture, and it produces exactly the same behaviour.

Each deposit converts over its own year — rates add, they don't queue

start6 months1 year

Topping up mid-way never delays what you were already converting. Add more at month six and the original finishes on its original schedule while the new amount runs its own.

Where the supply comes from

Zero at genesis. Not a small premine, not a treasury allocation, not a founder tranche vesting over eighteen months — zero. Every SWIPE that will ever exist is minted by a payment being processed, and no function exists that would let anyone create one another way.

A merchant is minted one SWIPE per dollar of volume. Stakers are emitted esSWIPE at a fixed ratio to the fee, alongside the USDC. That's the entire monetary policy, and it can't be changed by anyone including whoever deployed it.

A $2,450 invoice

Merchant receives2,388.75 USDC, immediately
Stakers receive61.25 USDC, immediately
Merchant is minted2,450 SWIPE, immediately and unrestricted
Stakers are emitted61.25 esSWIPE, pro-rata, converting over a year

Which means a merchant who stakes what they were minted eventually collects part of everyone else's fees — including their own. Process enough and the effective rate falls below 2.5%.

Why emissions track fees instead of time

Most staking tokens emit on a clock — so much per block, forever, whether or not the thing is used. That pays people to hold during silence, and supply grows fastest exactly when it's least deserved.

Here emissions are a multiple of fees collected. A month with no payments emits nothing. There is no schedule to front-run, no cliff, and no way for supply to grow without somebody having actually paid for something.

The bug the simulation caught

The standard fee-accumulator pattern has a rounding flaw that ships in production contracts constantly. Per-share accounting floors when a fee is deposited, but each staker's own multiplication can round the other way — so the last person to claim from a pool is owed a unit or two more than the contract actually holds. Their transaction reverts, and the final claimant can never withdraw.

Across 20,000 randomized pools, 2,213 hit it. The fix is to pay the lesser of what's owed and what's held, so the shortfall — at most a few millionths of a dollar — is dropped rather than turning into a permanently stuck balance. After the fix: zero.

What this isn't

Not a card network. No chargebacks, disputes, refunds it can enforce, fraud screening, or compliance of any kind. Those are most of what a real processor sells.

Not solving the hard problem. The customer still needs USDC. That's the actual barrier and no contract design fixes it.

No subsidy, which cuts both ways. Nothing was allocated to bootstrap this — no liquidity mining, no marketing allocation, no reason for anyone to be the first merchant. That makes the launch honest and makes adoption genuinely harder. Both things are true.

Not audited. Verified against simulations, which is not the same thing, and running on a testnet with play money.

Open a merchant account, pay yourself from a second wallet, then stake and watch the fee arrive as claimable USDC. The whole loop takes a few minutes and costs a fraction of a cent.
